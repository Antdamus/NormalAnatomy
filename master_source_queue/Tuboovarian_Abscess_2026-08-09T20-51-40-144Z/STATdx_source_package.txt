TITLE: Pelvic Inflammatory Disease

KEY FACTS
- Terminology
  - Infection of upper female genital tract
- Imaging
  - Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) is most often clinical diagnosis
  - Primary goal of imaging is to evaluate for complications and potential treatment planning
  - Imaging may be normal in early or uncomplicated PID
  - Pyosalpinx: Echogenic debris within fallopian tube
    - Cogwheel sign: Thickened longitudinal folds of fallopian tube have nodular appearance when viewed in cross section
  - Tuboovarian/pelvic abscesses
  - Inflammatory changes in surrounding fat with obscuration of soft tissue planes
  - Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome: Inflammation of RUQ peritoneal surfaces from infection extending up paracolic gutter
- Top Differential Diagnoses
  - Perforated appendicitis
  - Ovarian torsion
  - Ruptured dermoid
- Pathology
  - Sexually transmitted disease most commonly caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae or Chlamydia trachomatis
    - 30-40% are polymicrobial
- Clinical Issues
  - Common symptoms include vaginal discharge, pelvic pain, fever, and increased white blood cell count
  - Long-term sequelae
    - 10-15% of women may become infertile
    - Increased risk of ectopic pregnancy
  - Antibiotic therapy for uncomplicated PID
  - Percutaneous or open drainage of abscesses

TERMINOLOGY
- Abbreviations
  - Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)
  - Tuboovarian abscess (TOA)
- Definitions
  - PID: Collective term for acute pyogenic inflammatory disease of upper female genital tract, encompassing inflammation of endometrium (endometritis), cervix (cervicitis), fallopian tubes (salpingitis), ovaries (oophoritis), and peritoneal cavity (peritonitis)
  - TOA: Severe end of PID spectrum resulting in destruction of normal structures with formation of inflammatory mass encompassing both fallopian tube and ovary

Imaging
- General Features
  - Most often clinical diagnosis
  - Primary goal of imaging is to evaluate for complications and potential treatment planning
  - There is considerable overlap in imaging features of pathologic entities on PID spectrum because of gradual progression from 1 to next more severe condition
  - Best diagnostic clue
    - Findings of pelvic inflammation common to all entities on PID spectrum
      - Thickening of uterosacral ligaments, obliteration of fascial planes
      - Pelvic fat infiltration, edema, or haziness
        - 1 of most sensitive findings of acute PID, seen in ~ 65% of patients
      - Peritoneal thickening, enhancement ± free pelvic fluid
    - Salpingitis
      - Tubular adnexal mass with wall thickening ± mild luminal distention with fluid
    - Pyosalpinx
      - Dilated fallopian tube with mural enhancement and low-level echoes
    - Oophoritis
      - Enlarged hyperemic polycystic ovaries with multiple small follicles measuring 2-10 mm
        - Ovarian short-axis diameter > 3 cm
      - Overlap with normal ovarian appearance, making it challenging to diagnose oophoritis based solely on ovarian findings
    - TOA
      - Fallopian tube and ovary not identified as discrete entities; rather, together form tuboovarian complex
      - Nonspecific solid and cystic complex adnexal mass
        - Internal septations and mural irregularity
        - ± fluid-debris level
        - Rarely, internal gas
      - Often loss of fat planes between mass and adjacent pelvic organs
      - Secondary involvement of adjacent structures
        - Ileus, bowel obstruction, or reactive bowel wall thickening
        - Ureteropelvicaliectasis (functional or mechanical obstruction)
        - Intraperitoneal abscess if TOA ruptures
  - Location
    - Unilateral or bilateral
- Ultrasonographic Findings
  - Salpingitis and pyosalpinx
    - May be painful when probe touches cervix
      - US equivalent of cervical motion tenderness on clinical exam
    - Thick-walled, tubular adnexal structure (salpingitis), distended with echogenic fluid and debris (pyosalpinx)
    - ± fluid-debris level
    - Thick, hypervascular wall ≥ 5 mm
    - Cogwheel sign
      - Thickened longitudinal endosalpingeal folds of fallopian tube have nodular appearance when viewed in cross section
      - Sensitive marker of acute disease
    - Incomplete septa
      - Created by distended fallopian tube folding back on itself
  - Oophoritis
    - Polycystic ovarian enlargement and indistinct contours
    - Parovarian fluid
    - Normal to increased flow on color Doppler
  - Endometritis
    - Poor definition of endometrial echo complex
    - Indefinite uterus sign
      - Inflammation and echogenic fluid in cul-de-sac obscure uterine margins, especially along posterior border (also seen on CT)
  - Cervicitis
    - Enlarged uterine cervix
    - Hyperemic endocervical canal and parametrial fat stranding
  - TOA
    - Multilocular complex cystic and solid adnexal mass
    - Thick wall and septations, mural irregularity
    - May have fluid-debris level
  - Pelvic inflammation
    - Increased echogenicity of pelvic fat with ill-defined uterus
    - Increased vascularity of peritubal/pelvic fat
- CT Findings
  - Salpingitis, pyosalpinx
    - May be normal in mild cases of salpingitis
    - Wall thickening and enhancement of fallopian tubes (usually > 5 mm)
      - CT finding of tubal thickening has high specificity of 95% for diagnosis of PID
    - Distention of tube with high-attenuation fluid
    - Enlarged, indistinct but separate ovaries
  - Oophoritis
    - Enlarged, edematous ovaries measuring > 3 cm
    - Polycystic-like appearance of ovaries with multiple small (2- to 10-mm) follicles
    - Increased stromal enhancement
    - Indistinct contours with preserved architecture
  - Endometritis
    - Increased size of uterus caused by inflammation
    - Accumulation of fluid in endometrial canal
    - Abnormal endometrial enhancement relative to surrounding inner myometrium caused by mucosal hyperemia
    - Loss of clear separation of uterus from adnexal and parametrial fat indistinct uterine border sign
  - Cervicitis
    - Enlarged uterine cervix with abnormally enhancing endocervical mucosa
  - TOA
    - Ill-defined, multilocular, cystic and solid adnexal mass
      - Thick, enhancing, irregular wall and septa
      - Internal gas uncommon but specific
  - Pelvic inflammation
    - Pelvic edema with fat stranding and fluid
      - Haziness/stranding of pelvic fat, obscuring fascial planes
      - Mild in early PID and more pronounced with advanced disease
    - Thickening and enhancement of peritoneum and uterine ligaments
    - Involvement of adjacent structures
      - Mural thickening of small/large bowel and urinary bladder
      - Small or large bowel ileus/obstruction
      - Ureteropelviectasis from functional or mechanical obstruction
      - Thrombophlebitis of pelvic vessels
        - May cause ovarian vein thrombosis
  - Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome
    - Complication of PID
    - Peritoneal spread of infection via right paracolic gutter to involve peritoneal surfaces of RUQ
    - Perihepatitis, inflammation of Glisson capsule
      - Thickening and enhancement of anterior liver capsule
      - Subcapsular and periportal geographic areas of hepatic perfusional variation
    - Gallbladder wall thickening and pericholecystic fluid
    - Fluid and stranding in right paracolic gutter
    - Peritoneal septa, loculated perihepatic fluid
- MR Findings
  - Salpingitis, pyosalpinx
    - T1WI
      - Tube contents: Hypointense to intermediate signal intensity
    - T2WI
      - Tube contents: Intermediate to high signal intensity
    - T1WI C+ FS
      - Wall thickening and hyperenhancement of fallopian tubes ± distention
  - TOA
    - T1WI
      - Ill-defined adnexal mass
        - Contains low-signal fluid
        - Hemorrhagic or proteinaceous material can be hyperintense
        - Thick, irregular wall
        - May have hyperintense rim along inner wall of abscess cavity due to granulation tissue and hemorrhage
    - T2WI
      - Ill-defined, heterogeneous adnexal mass
        - Contains intermediate- to high-signal fluid
        - Hypointense, thick, irregular wall and septations
        - Hyperintense edema in parametrial fat
        - Hypointense, linear, fibrous stranding in pelvic fat
    - T1WI C+
      - Septal and thick rim enhancement of adnexal mass
      - Mesh-like stranding in pelvic fat due to adhesions and fibrosis
    - DWI
      - TOA cavities have high signal intensity on DWI with low ADC
      - Very useful in differentiating TOA from other ovarian masses, particularly malignant tumors
  - Pelvic inflammation
    - T2WI
      - Hyperintense bands of inflammation and fluid in pelvic fat
    - T1WI C+ FS
      - Enhancing bands in pelvic fat
      - Peritoneal thickening and enhancement
  - Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome
    - MR may help if hepatic parenchymal abnormalities are confusing on CT
      - Subcapsular and periportal geographic areas of hypervascularity in late arterial phase
      - Isointense on delayed postcontrast sequences
      - No corresponding signal abnormality on precontrast sequences
- Imaging Recommendations
  - Best imaging tool
    - US for initial evaluation and follow-up, guidance for abscess/pyosalpinx drainage
    - CECT has sensitivity of 79% and specificity of 99%, while MR has sensitivity of 95% and specificity of 89% for diagnosis of PID
    - CT for complicated PID
      - Late arterial phase shown to be most sensitive for making diagnosis
    - MR most useful in setting of chronic PID
      - Helps differentiate hydrosalpinx vs. peritoneal inclusion cysts from adhesions vs. cystic ovarian masses
  - Best imaging tool
    - US
      - Initial modality in female patients with pelvic pain
      - Optimal for identification of ovaries and exclusion of torsion or ectopic pregnancy
    - CT
      - Useful if symptoms are nonspecific to exclude nongynecologic diagnoses
      - Helps to identify patients requiring hospitalization
      - Useful if known PID to assess for accessibility for percutaneous drainage
    - MR
      - Aids in identifying ovary
      - Demonstrates tubular nature of dilated fallopian tube to exclude ovarian malignancy
      - Helps differentiate pyosalpinx from hematosalpinx
      - Helps differentiate TOA from ovarian malignancy and endometriosis
  - Protocol advice
    - CT: IV and oral contrast timed to opacify distal small bowel
    - MR: Fat suppression on T2WI and T1WI C+ images improves visualization of inflammatory changes within pelvic fat

Differential Diagnosis
- Other Causes of Pelvic Infection
  - Appendicitis
    - Thick-walled, right pelvic tubular structure ± luminal distention with fluid and stranding of surrounding fat; may represent appendicitis or salpingitis/pyosalpinx
      - Clue is tracing this structure and trying to establish its connection to cecum or uterus
    - Dilated appendix > 6 mm ± appendicolith
    - Right pelvic inflammation
    - Cecum and terminal ileum may be thickened
    - Soft tissue stranding in mesoappendix and periappendiceal fat
    - May form right lower quadrant abscess
  - Diverticulitis
    - Usually left-sided involving sigmoid colon
    - Thickened colon wall
    - Usually obvious diverticulosis
    - May see bubbles of gas from perforated diverticula
    - May be complicated by abscess formation
    - Generally older age group than typical PID patient
  - Pelvic abscess from another cause
    - e.g., diverticulitis, Crohn, ruptured appendicitis
    - Complex cystic mass
    - Should have normal ovary or peripheral involvement of ovary
    - Unilateral rather than bilateral
    - Presence of gas is more common than in TOA
- Other Causes of Acute Pelvic Pain
  - Ovarian torsion
    - Enlarged, heterogeneous ovary with prominent peripheral follicles
    - Ovarian architecture is preserved
    - Whirlpool sign: Twisted vascular pedicle
    - Normal to diminished vascularity of adnexa
    - Mild or no pelvic inflammation
  - Ruptured dermoid
    - Cystic mass containing fat &/or calcifications
    - Extruded contents cause chemical peritonitis
      - Soft tissue inflammatory changes in pelvis
    - Ectopic location suggests torsion, predisposing factor for rupture
- Other Causes of Distended Fallopian Tube
  - Hydrosalpinx
    - Tube distended with simple fluid
    - No wall thickening or surrounding inflammation
  - Hematosalpinx
    - Avascular, blood-filled fallopian tube
    - No pelvic fat inflammation
    - Easily differentiated on MR
  - Ectopic pregnancy
    - Positive serum β-hCG
    - Generally rounded or oval-shaped, extraovarian, solid adnexal mass
    - No inflammation of fat
    - Unilateral
- Other Causes of Adnexal Mass
  - Ovarian neoplasm
    - Mixed solid-cystic lesion
    - No pelvic inflammation
    - Large amount of free fluid
    - ± peritoneal deposits
    - No clinical signs of pain and infection

Pathology
- General Features
  - Etiology
    - Ascending infection from vagina, which progresses to involve cervix, uterus, fallopian tubes, &/or ovaries
    - Most commonly due to bacterial infection
      - Sexually transmitted disease
      - Most common organisms are Neisseria gonorrhoeae or Chlamydia trachomatis
      - 30-40% are polymicrobial
      - Less common organisms include gram-negative rods, tuberculosis, actinomycosis
    - Risk factors
      - Multiple sex partners or partner with multiple sex partners
      - Intrauterine device
        - 3x increased risk of PID
        - PID occurs within 1st few months of insertion
        - Higher occurrence of actinomycosis
      - Prior uterine procedure (e.g., D&C, biopsy)
      - Young age
      - High coital frequency
      - Low socioeconomic status
    - Postmenopausal TOAs may be associated with concomitant gynecologic malignancy in up to 50% of cases
    - Rupture of TOA may cause life-threatening peritonitis
    - Hematogenous spread and extension of infection from adjacent organs are less common causes of PID
- Microscopic Features
  - Fibrosis, acute and chronic inflammatory changes
  - Sequelae include dense fibrosis and adhesions encompassing tubes, ovaries, and uterus

Clinical Issues
- Presentation
  - Most common signs/symptoms
    - Up to 35% of patients with PID are asymptomatic
    - In mild cases, symptoms may be vague and nonspecific
    - Mucopurulent vaginal discharge
    - Pelvic pain
    - Fever and elevated white blood cell count
    - Cervical motion and adnexal tenderness on exam
    - RUQ pain and abnormal liver function tests (Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome)
  - Absence of leukocytosis or normal CRP does not rule out diagnosis of PID
- Demographics
  - Age
    - Greatest incidence in young women (< 25 years)
      - Cervix in teenage girls not fully matured, increasing risk of ascending infection
  - Epidemiology
    - Most frequent gynecologic cause of emergency department visits
      - ~ 350,000 visits per year in United States
    - Affects ~ 1 million women annually in United States
    - Accounts for ~ 275,000 hospitalizations/year
- Natural History & Prognosis
  - Most respond to therapy
  - TOA is most severe presentation of PID, occurring in ~ 15-34% of patients with PID
  - Rupture of TOA can cause life-threatening peritonitis
  - Bowel obstruction due to adhesions
  - Causes tubal damage, scarring, occlusion, and hydrosalpinx formation
    - 6x increased risk of ectopic pregnancy
      - Salpingitis can result in salpingitis isthmica nodosum
    - 10-15% of women may become infertile
      - 8% after single episode of PID
      - 20% after 2 episodes
      - 40% after 3 episodes
    - Increased risk of future episodes of PID
    - Chronic pelvic pain in up to 20%
- Treatment
  - Antibiotic therapy for uncomplicated PID
  - Percutaneous drainage of abscesses
    - Transvaginal drainage often best approach for pelvic collections
      - Higher rate of catheter dislodgement than transabdominal
    - Other approaches include transabdominal, transgluteal, or transrectal depending on location of abscess
    - Catheter removal criteria
      - Resolution of fever and leukocytosis
      - Drainage output < 10-20 mL/day
      - No fistula or large cavity on catheter injection
  - In severe septic forms (generalized peritonitis, septic shock), surgery (laparoscopy or laparotomy) should be considered for drainage

Diagnostic Checklist
- Image Interpretation Pearls
  - Use different scanning planes with US and MR or multiplanar reformations with CT to prove cystic adnexal mass is actually dilated fallopian tube
