## Fused Teaching Backbone

### Key Facts
- [Both] Adnexal torsion is the more precise term because the fallopian tube is usually involved along with the ovary; isolated fallopian tube torsion is rare but important.
- [Both] The core mechanism is rotation of the ovary/adnexa on its vascular pedicle, causing venous and lymphatic congestion first, then arterial compromise, ischemia, hemorrhagic infarction, and necrosis if untreated.
- [Both] The most consistent imaging clue is an enlarged, edematous, abnormally positioned ovary, often with peripheralized follicles and variable Doppler flow.
- [Both] Normal arterial or even venous flow does not exclude torsion. Venous outflow is usually affected earliest; preserved central venous flow can suggest viability.
- [Both] The twisted vascular pedicle/whirlpool sign is highly specific, but it is not always seen; CT reports it in fewer than one third of cases, and ultrasound visualization depends on technique, degree of twist, and residual intrapedicular flow.
- [Both] Adult torsion commonly has a benign lead point, especially a physiologic follicular/corpus luteum cyst, dermoid/mature teratoma, paraovarian cyst, or other benign ovarian mass.
- [Both] Treatment is urgent surgery with detorsion when viable and salpingo-oophorectomy when necrotic/infarcted; remove any mass serving as the lead point.

### Terminology and Pathophysiology
- [RadPrimer] Adnexal torsion is more accurate than ovarian torsion because torsion usually includes the fallopian tube; isolated tubal torsion may occur.
- [STATdx] The pedicle twists around the infundibulopelvic and utero-ovarian ligament axis, causing venous/lymphatic congestion before arterial obstruction.
- [Both] Torsion of both ovary and fallopian tube is most commonly found at surgery. The twist may range from partial/incomplete torsion to multiple rotations.
- [RadPrimer] Sequential venous, lymphatic, and arterial obstruction produces central edema and microscopic hemorrhage early, peripheral displacement of follicles, and late hemorrhagic infarction.
- [STATdx] Necrotic ovaries are friable and may lose normal architecture; edematous or hemorrhagic ovaries may still be viable.

### Clinical Setting
- [Both] Classic presentation is acute severe pelvic pain, often sharp and unremitting, with nausea/vomiting; pain may be intermittent with torsion-detorsion.
- [Both] Torsion accounts for about 2-3% of gynecologic emergencies and is most common in reproductive-age patients.
- [RadPrimer] Risk increases during pregnancy, especially before 20 weeks, with ovarian stimulation, and after prior abdominopelvic surgery.
- [STATdx] Up to 20% of cases may occur in pregnant women; patients may be misdiagnosed clinically as appendicitis or nephrolithiasis.
- [Both] Fever can occur when the ovary is infarcted. A palpable adnexal mass may or may not be present.

## Imaging Features

### Ultrasound
- [Both] Best initial test: transvaginal/endovaginal ultrasound with grayscale and color Doppler. Compare both ovaries on grayscale and Doppler.
- [Both] Enlarged ovary: greater than 4 cm in longest dimension or greater than 20 cm3 in premenopausal patients; greater than 10 cm3 in postmenopausal patients. Torsed ovaries average about 28 times normal volume.
- [Both] Grayscale findings: enlarged rounded ovary, heterogeneous/hyperechoic edematous stroma, peripheral fluid-filled follicles, thickened echogenic follicle walls/follicular ring sign, probe tenderness, free fluid, and an underlying cystic or solid lead-point mass.
- [Both] Position: torsed ovary may be displaced midline, anterior/superior to the uterine fundus, cephalad, or into the cul-de-sac.
- [Both] Twisted pedicle signs: whirlpool sign on dynamic sweep/cine clip, target sign with concentric hypoechoic stripes, beaked twisted fallopian tube, or a heterogeneous tubular edematous tube.
- [RadPrimer] Isolated fallopian tube torsion may show an adnexal cyst separate from a normal ovary and a dilated/tender tube.
- [STATdx] Cine clips of the adnexa help identify the twisted vascular pedicle; the pedicle may be harder to see with less than 360 degrees of twist or when intrapedicular vessels no longer flow.

### Doppler Pitfalls and Viability
- [Both] Doppler findings vary with acuity and degree of obstruction. Normal arterial and venous waveforms may be present in acute or incomplete torsion.
- [Both] Venous flow is usually affected first; absent venous flow in an enlarged echogenic ovary with peripheral follicles is an early reliable sign.
- [Both] Dual ovarian arterial supply means arterial flow can persist; normal arterial flow does not exclude torsion.
- [STATdx] Absent or reversed arterial diastolic flow and low-velocity/parvus-tardus arterial waveforms have been described.
- [Both] Absence of flow in the vascular pedicle or absent ovarian enhancement raises concern for necrosis/infarction; central venous flow favors viability.

### CT
- [Both] CT can suggest torsion when an enlarged, displaced ovary is seen, especially with midline/superior location, deviation of the uterus toward the torsed side, hypodense edematous stroma, peripheral cysts, free fluid, or an associated lead-point lesion.
- [Both] The twisted vascular pedicle is the most specific CT sign but is seen in fewer than one third of cases; use sagittal/coronal multiplanar reformations.
- [RadPrimer] Noncontrast CT can show ovarian hematoma or hematosalpinx greater than 50 HU.
- [Both] Heterogeneous, minimal, or absent enhancement indicates progression from ischemia to infarction/necrosis.

### MRI
- [Both] MRI can confirm torsion and evaluate underlying mass or infarction when ultrasound is equivocal or when CT/MR is obtained for acute abdomen/pelvic pain.
- [Both] T1-weighted images: stromal edema is low signal; T1 hyperintensity suggests hemorrhagic infarction, hematosalpinx, or hemorrhagic lead-point lesion.
- [Both] T2-weighted images: edematous stroma is high signal with peripheral hyperintense follicles; dark signal may indicate infarcted/nonviable tissue.
- [Both] Contrast-enhanced T1: enhancement varies with chronicity and infarction; absent stromal enhancement supports infarction. MRI can show the twisted pedicle and relationship to the uterus/adnexa.

## Differential Diagnosis
- [Both] Hemorrhagic corpus luteum/cyst: most common false positive. Look for an otherwise normal ovary with preserved vascularity, reticular/fishnet fibrin strands, retractile clot, fluid-fluid or fluid-debris level, and rim flow.
- [Both] Ectopic pregnancy: positive beta-hCG, no intrauterine pregnancy, adnexal mass separate from ovary, tubal ring/ring of fire, yolk sac/embryo when visible, and hemoperitoneum when ruptured.
- [Both] Pelvic inflammatory disease: thickened dilated tubes, pyosalpinx with low-level echoes/fluid-fluid level, hyperemia, enlarged ovaries from oophoritis, tubo-ovarian abscess, complex adnexal inflammatory mass, and possible indefinite uterus sign.
- [Both] Isolated fallopian tube torsion: dilated thick-walled tube with internal debris/hemorrhage and normal ovaries; whirlpool sign can still be present.
