## Fused Key Facts
- [Both] Epididymitis/orchitis is infectious or inflammatory involvement of the epididymis, testis, or both. Orchitis alone is less common; secondary orchitis occurs by contiguous spread from epididymitis.
- [RadPrimer] Epididymitis often begins in the epididymal tail, progresses through the body, and may extend to the testis. Orchitis complicates roughly 20-40% of epididymitis.
- [Both] Color Doppler ultrasound with a high-frequency linear transducer is the test of choice. The core imaging clue is enlargement plus hyperemia of the epididymis and/or testis, ideally compared with the contralateral side.
- [RadPrimer] Acute epididymitis is typically enlarged, relatively hypoechoic, and coarse/heterogeneous from edema and hemorrhage; chronic epididymitis may become enlarged and hyperechoic.
- [STATdx] Pediatric cases often show a reactive hydrocele, scrotal wall thickening, and whole-hemiscrotum inflammatory change. In infants and younger children, consider underlying GU anomalies.
- [RadPrimer] Severe epididymoorchitis can compromise venous outflow and produce ischemia, infarction, pyocele, epididymal abscess, testicular abscess, or later testicular atrophy.
- [Both] Management is usually antibiotics plus supportive care; follow-up imaging is important if symptoms do not improve or abscess is suspected.

## Terminology
- [RadPrimer] Synonyms include acute scrotum, orchitis, and epididymoorchitis.
- [STATdx] Epididymoorchitis is infectious inflammation of the epididymis, testicle, or both; isolated orchitis is much less common.
- [Both] In future narrative, keep the RadPrimer title Epididymitis/Orchitis for deck routing while noting STATdx uses Epididymoorchitis.

## Imaging Synthesis
### Ultrasound Protocol
- [Both] Use a high-frequency linear transducer and color/power Doppler. Compare symptomatic and asymptomatic sides, especially when increased vascularity is subtle.
- [STATdx] Transverse side-by-side Doppler views are especially useful for overall size, symmetry, flow, and echotexture comparison.
- [STATdx] Supporting the scrotum on towels may help; testicular flow can be difficult to demonstrate in very young males but becomes easier in inflammatory hyperemia.

### Grayscale Ultrasound
- [RadPrimer] Epididymitis: focal tail enlargement or diffuse epididymal enlargement; acute disease is often hypoechoic/coarse/heterogeneous.
- [RadPrimer] Orchitis: focal hypoechoic intratesticular areas adjacent to an enlarged epididymis or diffuse testicular enlargement/heterogeneity; severe infection can thicken the tunica albuginea.
- [Both] Reactive hydrocele is common; complex low-level echoes, septa, tunical thickening, scrotal skin edema, and periorchitis support inflammation.
- [STATdx] Scrotal wall thickening and whole-hemiscrotal inflammatory change are common pediatric clues.

### Doppler Ultrasound
- [Both] Hyperemia of the epididymis, testis, or both is the central discriminator from torsion, but intermittent torsion can occasionally appear hyperemic.
- [RadPrimer] Low-resistance arterial flow is expected in inflammation; testicular RI below 0.5 supports inflammation relative to normal RI at or above 0.5.
- [RadPrimer] Focal avascular regions within hyperemic tissue suggest focal infarction. Reversal of arterial diastolic flow is an ominous sign associated with venous infarction.
- [STATdx] Power Doppler may make markedly increased flow more conspicuous.

### Additional Modalities
- [RadPrimer] Tc-99m scintigraphy historically helps differentiate torsion from epididymitis; epididymitis shows increased flow/perfusion and curvilinear activity in the hemiscrotum.
- [STATdx] Nuclear scintigraphy is now mostly historical because ultrasound has replaced it, but technique matters when used: secure the penis out of the field, support the scrotum, use pinhole collimation when possible, and image flow plus delayed phases.
- [STATdx] VCUG may be performed in infants and nonsexually active boys to evaluate ectopic ureter, reflux, urethral abnormality with reflux into the vas deferens, or high-pressure voiding patterns.
- [STATdx] MR urography may help if complex GU anomalies remain unclear after ultrasound and VCUG; MRI can also reveal hemiscrotal inflammatory edema in confusing presentations.

## Differential Diagnosis
- [Both] Testicular torsion: absent or diminished unilateral flow, possible twisted spermatic cord, surgical emergency. Epididymis may enlarge but lacks expected inflammatory vascularity.
- [STATdx] Intermittent torsion can be tricky because hyperemia may occur after detorsion; look for abnormal lie, bell-clapper hydrocele pattern, and high-resistance waveforms when pain has improved.
- [STATdx] Torsion of appendage testis/epididymis: nodular avascular appendage adjacent to testis with surrounding hyperemia; common pediatric acute scrotum mimic and may trigger reactive epididymal hyperemia.
- [RadPrimer] Testicular lymphoma: often large, heterogeneous, bilateral or disseminated, with epididymal/spermatic cord involvement; hemorrhage and necrosis are rare.
- [Both] Trauma/rupture: history of trauma, focal heterogeneity or avascular injury, contour deformity, tunica interruption, hematocele.
- [STATdx] Scrotal cellulitis: wall thickening/hyperemia with normal testis and epididymis.
- [STATdx] Hernia: bowel or abdominal contents continuous with the inguinal canal, with bowel wall signature/peristalsis and displacement of scrotal structures.
- [STATdx] Leukemia: unilateral or bilateral testicular enlargement, diffuse hypoechoic parenchyma, and palisading vessels.

## Pathology and Mechanism
- [Both] Young sexually active males commonly have ascending infection from Neisseria gonorrhoeae or Chlamydia trachomatis.
- [RadPrimer] Prepubertal boys, men older than 35, and men who practice anal intercourse more often have enteric organisms such as E. coli; other causes include Pseudomonas, Klebsiella, Proteus, Staphylococcus aureus, tuberculosis, and mumps.
- [STATdx] Pediatric disease may reflect direct seeding in GU anomalies or hematogenous spread; consider ectopic ureter, ectopic vas deferens, prostatic utricle, urethral duplication, posterior urethral valves, urethrorectal fistula, vesicoureteral reflux, neurogenic bladder, and anorectal malformation.
- [STATdx] Viral mumps orchitis may follow parotiditis by 3-5 days; mumps outbreaks can occur despite MMR vaccination, and epididymal-head swelling may predominate.
- [RadPrimer] Traumatic, chemical, post-exertional, and post-surgical epididymitis can mimic infectious epididymitis; traumatic epididymitis should not be treated as infectious disease when the clinical context supports trauma.

## Clinical Issues and Management
- [Both] Typical presentation: gradual or acute scrotal pain, swelling, erythema, fever, dysuria, urinary symptoms, or urethral discharge.
- [RadPrimer] Prehn sign may help clinically: epididymitis pain often improves with scrotal elevation, while torsion pain may worsen or not improve. Do not use this sign to replace urgent Doppler evaluation.
- [RadPrimer] Pyuria is common; elevated C-reactive protein can help distinguish epididymoorchitis from torsion in the cited RadPrimer summary.
- [STATdx] Instrumentation, indwelling catheters, clean intermittent catheterization, urethritis, or prostatitis can coexist or predispose.
- [Both] Treat with antibiotics when bacterial infection is suspected or proven, plus bed rest, scrotal support/elevation, analgesics, anti-inflammatory agents, and ice packs when appropriate.
- [STATdx] In pediatric culture-negative cases, viral etiology may be presumed and antibiotic use may be selective.
- [Both] Work up GU anomalies in younger children and recurrent cases. Surgical exploration is reserved for unresolved torsion concern or complications such as abscess, pyocele, or infarction.
- [RadPrimer] Follow-up scans are indicated if the patient does not improve, especially to exclude abscess.

## Diagnostic Checklist
- [Both] First decide whether torsion is safely excluded; low or absent testicular flow remains torsion until proven otherwise.
- [Both] Look for enlarged epididymis/testis plus hyperemia, low-resistance flow, reactive hydrocele, scrotal wall thickening, and contralateral asymmetry.
- [RadPrimer] Search for complications: focal avascular regions, reversed diastolic flow, abscess, pyocele, infarction, and late atrophy.
- [STATdx] In young children and recurrent cases, search beyond the scrotum for GU anomaly risk.
