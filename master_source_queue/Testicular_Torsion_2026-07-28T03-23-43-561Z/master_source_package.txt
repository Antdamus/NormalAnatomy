KEY FACTS
- [Both] Testicular torsion is spontaneous or traumatic twisting of the testis and spermatic cord within the scrotum, causing venous obstruction first and then arterial compromise with ischemia or infarction.
- [Both] The best diagnostic clue is absent, decreased, or asymmetric testicular blood flow on color Doppler US; comparison with the contralateral testis is mandatory.
- [Both] The whirlpool sign is spiral twisting of the spermatic cord cranial to the testis and epididymis, producing a torsion knot or concentric-layer pattern.
- [STATdx] Heterogeneous diffuse or patchy hypoechoic parenchyma predicts nonviability. Testicular fragmentation reflects hemorrhage between lobules; testicular patching reflects islands of necrosis.
- [Both] Normal grayscale and even normal Doppler flow do not exclude early, partial, or intermittent torsion when clinical suspicion remains high.
- [Both] Testicular salvage depends on time and degree of twist. Most testes are viable when treated within 6 hours; salvage falls sharply after 12 hours and is essentially absent after more than 24 hours.
- [STATdx] CEUS or MR can help in equivocal cases, but imaging must not delay prompt surgical management.

TERMINOLOGY
- [Both] Spermatic cord torsion/testicular torsion means twisting of the testis and spermatic cord with vascular occlusion and possible infarction.
- [RadPrimer] Torsion may be complete or incomplete and is unilateral in about 95% of patients.

IMAGING
General approach
- [Both] Use high-frequency linear transducer ultrasound with grayscale plus color/power Doppler; compare both testes for echogenicity, size, lie, color flow, and waveforms.
- [Both] Optimize Doppler for slow flow with low pulse repetition frequency, low wall filter, and high Doppler gain.
- [STATdx] Pulsed/spectral Doppler has a limited but useful adjunctive role in partial torsion; side-to-side waveform shape and velocity comparison may expose equivocal asymmetry.

Grayscale ultrasound
- [Both] Early torsion may have normal grayscale echogenicity; this appearance favors viability if absent flow is present and symptoms are short.
- [Both] Enlarged heterogeneous testis and epididymis, scrotal wall edema, secondary hydrocele, hemorrhage, necrosis, and fragmentation suggest longer duration or missed torsion.
- [RadPrimer] At around 24 hours, congestion, hemorrhage, and infarction become evident.
- [STATdx] Chronic torsion may show an atrophic hypoechoic testis with relatively enlarged echogenic epididymis.

Color and power Doppler
- [Both] Absent or decreased intratesticular flow is central for diagnosis. In acute torsion, Doppler sensitivity is about 80-90%, and early/partial torsion may be falsely normal.
- [RadPrimer] In partial torsion of 360 degrees or less, spectral Doppler may show diminished diastolic arterial flow.
- [STATdx] Partial/intermittent torsion may show normal, decreased, or absent flow during pain and hyperemia after detorsion, potentially mimicking orchitis.
- [Both] Subacute or missed torsion may show no intratesticular flow with increased flow in paratesticular tissues, epididymis-cord complex, dartos fascia, tunica, or scrotal wall.

Whirlpool sign and spermatic cord
- [Both] Directly seeing a twisted spermatic cord is highly specific for torsion and is especially helpful when intratesticular flow is equivocal or preserved.
- [STATdx] In transverse plane, the torsion knot can look like a pseudomass or concentric knot; in longitudinal plane, the cord appears angulated or knot-like rather than smooth and linear.
- [STATdx] High-resistance flow may be seen in the spermatic cord proximal to the twist, and the ultrasound appearance may help guide manual detorsion direction.

CEUS, MR, and nuclear medicine
- [STATdx] CEUS: normal testes enhance quickly and avidly; complete torsion lacks enhancement; partial torsion enhances slowly or less than the contralateral side. CEUS can also identify segmental or global infarction from nontorsion causes.
- [Both] MR can show decreased perfusion and a whirlpool pattern but should be rare problem-solving only and should not delay surgery.
- [RadPrimer] Tc-99m pertechnetate can show reduced/absent flow, a rounded cold area, and dartos halo, but [STATdx] notes nuclear medicine has fallen out of favor and is not recommended by ACR appropriateness guidance when US is available.

DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS
- [Both] Torsion of appendix testis/epididymis: common in prepubertal boys; blue-dot sign; hyperechoic appendage with central hypoechoic area, reactive hydrocele, skin thickening, and peripheral hyperemia.
- [STATdx] Testicular infarction: can lack flow like torsion but lacks whirlpool sign; may be global or segmental and is often treated conservatively rather than surgically.
- [Both] Epididymoorchitis/abscess: enlarged hypoechoic epididymis with increased flow, thick-walled hypoechoic focus with internal echoes, and possible segmental/global venous infarction that can mimic torsion.
- [Both] Trauma: hematocele, rupture/irregular contour, heterogeneous parenchyma, and avascular fracture plane; trauma can rarely precipitate torsion.
- [Both] Tumor: focal hypoechoic heterogeneous mass, usually less painful, with abnormal vascularity rather than complete avascularity.

PATHOLOGY
- [Both] Most torsion is spontaneous; traumatic torsion is rare.
- [Both] Bell clapper deformity reflects deficient posterior fixation by the tunica vaginalis/gubernaculum, allowing the testis to rotate within the scrotum.
- [RadPrimer] Intravaginal torsion is the common pubertal type, with the knot within the tunica vaginalis; extravaginal torsion occurs outside the tunica vaginalis and is neonatal/perinatal.
- [STATdx] Bell clapper deformity is often bilateral, and contralateral orchiopexy is performed because the opposite testis is also at risk.
- [Both] Pathology ranges from venous congestion and edema to hemorrhage, ischemic necrosis, fibrosis, and a purple/black infarcted testis.

CLINICAL ISSUES AND MANAGEMENT
- [Both] Classic presentation is an adolescent or infant boy with acute scrotal/inguinal pain, swollen erythematous hemiscrotum, pain not relieved by elevation, absent cremasteric reflex, and abnormal testis lie.
- [STATdx] Younger prepubertal children may present atypically with groin or lower abdominal pain only, increasing misdiagnosis and risk of testis loss.
- [Both] Torsion is a surgical emergency. Treatment is surgical exploration, detorsion, and orchiopexy if viable; nonviable testis is removed.
- [STATdx] Contralateral orchiopexy is always performed because a similar anatomic predisposition is likely.
- [Both] Salvage rates: approximately 80-100% when treated before 6 hours, 76% at 6-12 hours, 20% at 12-24 hours, and 0% after 24 hours.

DIAGNOSTIC CHECKLIST
- [Both] Do not dismiss torsion because grayscale US is normal, especially in early, partial, or intermittent torsion.
- [Both] Search directly for the spermatic cord whirlpool sign above the testis when Doppler findings are equivocal.
- [Both] Always compare with the contralateral testis and optimize Doppler settings for low flow.
- [STATdx] Hyperemia after detorsion can mimic orchitis; correlate with intermittent pain history and symptom change.
