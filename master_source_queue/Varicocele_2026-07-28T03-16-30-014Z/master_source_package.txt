KEY FACTS
- [Both] Varicocele is dilation and tortuosity of veins of the pampiniform plexus, classically greater than 3 mm, caused by venous congestion and retrograde flow in the internal spermatic/testicular vein.
- [Both] Best imaging clue: dilated, serpiginous/tubular veins adjacent to or behind the superior pole of the testis, with increased color Doppler flow or reflux during Valsalva.
- [RadPrimer] Distribution: left sided is most common, right sided is uncommon, and bilateral disease is less common than isolated left-sided disease.
- [STATdx] Isolated right-sided varicocele is uncommon but is rarely associated with malignancy when no other concerning symptoms are present; retroperitoneal imaging may be unnecessary in otherwise reassuring adult cases, though US retroperitoneal assessment can be considered during the same exam.
- [Both] Valsalva is essential for small varicoceles; exam should include rest and Valsalva color Doppler.
- [STATdx] ESUR-style protocol: evaluate with and without Valsalva, supine and standing; measure the largest vein regardless of location; 3 mm or greater is diagnostic; report testicular volume when relevant.
- [Both] Varicocele is a frequent/treatable cause of male infertility, but many patients have normal semen parameters.

TERMINOLOGY
- [Both] Definition: dilation of pampiniform plexus veins greater than 3 mm due to retrograde flow in the testicular/internal spermatic vein.
- [STATdx] Dilated veins may involve the pampiniform plexus, cremasteric plexus, vein of vas deferens, and testicular/spermatic vein.
- [Both] Intratesticular varicocele is rare and involves dilated veins at the mediastinum testis; it must be distinguished from tubular ectasia of the rete testis.

IMAGING
- [Both] Ultrasound with color Doppler is the best diagnostic tool. Use high-frequency scrotal US with supine and standing assessment when possible.
- [Both] Grayscale US shows hypoechoic, tubular, serpiginous vessels superior/posterior/lateral to the testis; they may extend along the spermatic cord.
- [Both] Diagnostic size threshold: multiple pampiniform plexus veins greater than or equal to 3 mm, especially upright or during Valsalva.
- [Both] Color Doppler shows distention and increased flow/reflux with Valsalva. Slow flow can be missed unless low Doppler settings are used.
- [STATdx] If the Doppler scale is set appropriately, retrograde flow is directed toward the testis; reflux longer than 2 seconds during Valsalva is diagnostic of significant reflux.
- [STATdx] Slow venous flow can appear echogenic and mobile in real time and should not be mistaken for thrombus; true thrombosis is echogenic material in a noncompressible lumen without flow during Valsalva.
- [RadPrimer] MRI is usually incidental and shows serpiginous enhancing channels; slow flow can be intermediate T1 and high T2 signal, while higher velocity flow may create signal voids.
- [Both] Catheter venography demonstrates dilated venous channels and can document gonadal/testicular vein reflux; treatment may be catheter embolization.
- [STATdx] CT or MR may show enhancing tubular structures or reveal secondary venous causes such as renal vein thrombosis, retroperitoneal tumor, fibrosis, or systemic venous congestion.
- [RadPrimer] Scan the retroperitoneum for mass when clinical context suggests a secondary cause, especially recent-onset varicocele in an older patient.

DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS
- [STATdx] Hernia: echogenic fat or bowel displaces the testis, may accentuate with Valsalva, shows gut signature/peristalsis, and has minimal bowel-content Doppler flow.
- [Both] Epididymitis: enlarged heterogeneous epididymis with increased Doppler flow, separate from the pampiniform plexus, and without Valsalva-dependent varicocele reflux behavior.
- [Both] Tubular ectasia of the rete testis: avascular dilated tubules in the mediastinum testis; no Doppler flow, unlike intratesticular varicocele.
- [STATdx] Spermatocele/epididymal cyst: one or more anechoic or hypoechoic cysts without Doppler flow.
- [STATdx] Rare vascular lesions: testicular AVM, hemangioma, or high-flow extratesticular mass.
- [RadPrimer] Testicular torsion: enlarged hypoechoic testis with absent or decreased intratesticular flow.

PATHOLOGY AND MECHANISM
- [Both] Primary varicocele is related to venous valve incompetence and reflux, classically near the left renal vein/IVC junction.
- [Both] Left-sided predominance is explained by the longer left testicular vein and its perpendicular drainage into the left renal vein; the right testicular vein drains more directly into the IVC.
- [Both] Secondary causes include obstruction or compression of testicular venous drainage by renal/adrenal tumor, retroperitoneal nodes, lymphoma, RCC, retroperitoneal fibrosis, renal vein thrombosis, or nutcracker-type SMA compression.
- [Both] Potential fertility injury mechanisms include increased scrotal/testicular heat, oxidative stress, stagnant blood/ischemia, increased venous pressure with decreased arterial inflow, catecholamine reflux, and androgen deprivation.
- [RadPrimer] Clinical grading: small is palpable only with Valsalva, moderate is palpable with standing, and large is visible through scrotal skin and palpable while standing.

CLINICAL ISSUES
- [Both] Many varicoceles are asymptomatic and detected on physical exam or infertility work-up.
- [Both] Typical symptoms are vague scrotal discomfort, pressure, or heaviness, usually worse while standing.
- [Both] Varicocele is common in the general male population and more common among infertile men, but many men with varicocele have normal fertility and normal semen parameters.
- [STATdx] In adults, repair is generally considered for men trying to conceive who have palpable varicocele and abnormal semen parameters, or for scrotal pain.
- [STATdx] There is no indication to treat subclinical varicoceles found only by imaging; men with azoospermia may not benefit. Adolescent treatment is controversial and may use testicular volume discrepancy.
- [Both] Treatment options include catheter embolization/sclerotherapy and surgical ligation. STATdx notes that varicocelectomy is a misnomer because veins are ligated rather than removed.

DIAGNOSTIC CHECKLIST
- [Both] Perform Valsalva; small varicoceles may be occult without it.
- [Both] Confirm venous structure, diameter, and reflux rather than relying on gray-scale serpiginous appearance alone.
- [STATdx] Use Doppler settings sensitive enough for slow flow and reflux; avoid mistaking very slow flow for thrombosis.
- [RadPrimer] In an older man with new-onset varicocele, consider left renal vein occlusion or retroperitoneal/renal/adrenal tumor.
- [STATdx] In adults with isolated right-sided varicocele and no other malignancy symptoms, recent experience does not support frequent cancer association; in children younger than 9 years, look for tumor.
- [Both] Measure/report testicular volume when fertility or adolescent testicular function is part of the question.
