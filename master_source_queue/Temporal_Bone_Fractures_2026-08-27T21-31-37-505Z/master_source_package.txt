=== DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS BACKBONE ===
[Both]
- Pseudofractures: sutures, fissures, canaliculi, and aqueducts. Favor a pseudofracture when the lucency is bilateral/symmetric and has smooth corticated margins.
- Incus interposition procedure: surgical remodeling/realignment of the incus can mimic chronic incus dislocation.
- Mastoiditis or mastoid/middle-ear effusion: preexisting opacification may not represent acute hemotympanum or CSF leak.

=== ESSENTIAL TEACHING POINTS ===
[Both] Classify temporal bone fractures by otic capsule status because otic-capsule-violating fractures better predict sensorineural hearing loss, facial nerve injury, and CSF leak than the older longitudinal/transverse system.
[Both] Longitudinal fractures parallel the petrous ridge long axis and commonly involve the external auditory canal, middle ear, and ossicles while usually sparing the otic capsule.
[Both] Transverse fractures are perpendicular to the petrous ridge long axis and more often involve the otic capsule and facial nerve, with less frequent external auditory canal or middle-ear involvement.
[Both] Many fractures are oblique or mixed, so report both the anatomic orientation and whether the otic capsule is violated.
[Both] Secondary signs include hemotympanum, pneumolabyrinth, and intracranial or extracranial air/hemorrhage near the mastoid. Unexplained parapharyngeal space air should prompt a search for mastoid fracture.
[Both] Ossicular dislocations are more common than ossicular fractures; incudostapedial disruption is most common, followed by incudomalleolar disruption and complete incus dislocation.
[Both] The most clinically important structures to assess are the facial nerve canal, otic capsule, tegmen, carotid canal, and ossicles; also evaluate intracranial contents and cervical spine injuries.
[Both] Consider CTA/MRA if the fracture reaches the carotid canal, and CTV/MRV if the fracture reaches the dural venous sinus or jugular foramen region.
[Both] MR can help assess hemolabyrinth, facial nerve or membranous labyrinth enhancement, subacute intracranial complications, ICA injury, venous thrombosis, and suspected posttraumatic labyrinthitis ossificans.

=== CLINICAL AND MANAGEMENT POINTS ===
[Both] Common clinical findings include Battle sign, external auditory canal hemorrhage, hemotympanum, conductive hearing loss, sensorineural hearing loss, facial nerve dysfunction, CSF leak, and vertigo.
[Both] Conductive hearing loss initially may reflect hemotympanum or tympanic membrane injury; persistent conductive loss should prompt careful ossicular evaluation.
[Both] Immediate complete facial paralysis has a worse recovery prognosis than delayed paresis; delayed paresis often reflects reversible injury and is commonly observed, sometimes with steroids.
[Both] Most CSF leaks resolve spontaneously within 7 days, but persistent leaks may require surgery. Meningitis can occur in a minority of patients with CSF leak.
[Both] Severe head injury management takes priority. Carotid injury may require anticoagulation and/or endovascular therapy. Antibiotics are used when CSF leak is present.
