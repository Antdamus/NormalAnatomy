## Fused Teaching Backbone

### Definition And Anatomic Location

- [Both] Hydrocele is congenital or acquired serous fluid contained within the layers of the scrotal tunica vaginalis.
- [Both] The fluid is intrascrotal, external to the testis and epididymis, and not within the scrotal wall.
- [Both] The key anatomic clue is fluid surrounding the testis except at the posterior bare area, where the tunica vaginalis does not cover the testis and is attached to the epididymis.
- [Both] The specific location is the tunica vaginalis; simple hydrocele appears as avascular fluid, while chronic or complicated hydrocele may have echoes, debris, septations, or thickened tunica.

### Imaging Approach

- [Both] High-resolution scrotal ultrasound with a 9-15 MHz transducer is the modality of choice.
- [Both] Acute hydrocele usually appears as crescentic anechoic fluid surrounding the anterolateral testis, often displacing the testis posteromedially.
- [Both] Chronic hydrocele can show low-level mobile echoes, presumed cholesterol crystals, inflammatory debris, diffuse scrotal wall thickening, parietal calcifications, scrotoliths, and avascular septations.
- [Both] Color or power Doppler should show no vascularity within the hydrocele fluid or septations; power Doppler may demonstrate movement of internal debris and can help avoid mistaking debris artifact for flow.
- [Both] MRI is a problem-solving tool when extent or associated postoperative/complex findings need clarification: simple fluid is low signal on T1-weighted images, high signal on T2-weighted images, and nonenhancing; chronic or complex fluid may be intermediate on T2-weighted images.

### Etiology And Pathology

- [Both] Congenital or communicating hydrocele results from failure of the processus vaginalis to close.
- [Both] Acquired hydrocele can follow epididymitis, torsion, trauma, surgery, or rarely tumor; secondary hydroceles in adults should prompt attention to the underlying cause.
- [Both] Simple hydrocele is a fluid collection within the tunica vaginalis. Chronic cases may develop thickened tunica and septations, and acute infection or postinfectious change can make the fluid complex.
- [Both] Scrotal hematoma may resolve slowly and can appear as a complex collection that overlaps the imaging differential.

### Differential Diagnosis

- [Both] Pyocele: septated fluid with low-level internal echoes, usually with epididymitis, intrascrotal abscess, and clinical inflammatory signs.
- [Both] Hematocele: complex echogenic fluid in the tunica vaginalis, usually associated with trauma, torsion, or infarct.
- [Both] Scrotal hernia: peristalsing bowel or echogenic omentum within the scrotum rather than avascular fluid in the tunica vaginalis.
- [STATdx] The expanded image set reinforces practical recognition variants: thin septations with epididymo-orchitis, chronic low-level echoes/cholesterol-crystal appearance, encysted spermatic cord hydrocele, massive hydrocele, bilateral septated chronic hydroceles, and testicular compression.

### Clinical Issues And Treatment

- [Both] The typical presentation is an asymptomatic, painless scrotal mass or enlargement.
- [Both] Congenital hydroceles usually resolve by 18 months of age.
- [Both] About 25-50% of acquired hydroceles are associated with trauma, and about 10% of testicular tumors have an associated hydrocele.
- [Both] Treatment options include surgical hydrocelectomy, with open drainage and oversewing of hydrocele sac edges, or aspiration and sclerotherapy, which is less invasive but has a slightly higher recurrence risk.
