=== DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS BACKBONE ===
[RadPrimer]
Common:
- Normal postoperative pneumoperitoneum.
- Barotrauma.
- Iatrogenic injuries or complications, including feeding-tube injury and bowel anastomotic leak.
- Perforated ulcer, including duodenal and gastric ulcer.
- Diverticulitis.
- Intestinal trauma.
- Appendicitis.
- Thoracic mimics, including pneumothorax, subsegmental atelectasis, and cystic lung disease.
- Colonic interposition mimic.
- Subphrenic fat mimic.

Less common:
- Other causes of bowel perforation, including ileus/volvulus/obstruction, ischemia, vasculitis, infection/inflammatory bowel disease, malignancy, ingested foreign body, radiation therapy, fecal impaction, chemotherapy/steroids, and penetrating trauma.
- Peritonitis.
- Abdominal abscess.
- Pneumatosis cystoides intestinalis.
- Retrograde gas passage from the female genital tract.
- Small bowel diverticula.
- Foreign body perforation.

Alternative approach:
- Free intraperitoneal air in a neonate: necrotizing enterocolitis, cystic fibrosis-related GI disease, duodenal atresia/stenosis, and Hirschsprung disease.

=== ESSENTIAL TEACHING POINTS ===
[Both] CT is substantially more sensitive than plain radiographs for pneumoperitoneum and should be the test of choice when hollow viscus perforation is suspected.
[Both] Upright or decubitus radiographs are more sensitive than supine radiographs. Supine portable films often make free intraperitoneal gas subtle or difficult to see.
[Both] In unexplained free air, use the amount and location of gas to narrow the cause. Gas often localizes near the perforation site.
[Both] Gastric and duodenal perforations tend to produce upper abdominal free air above the transverse mesocolon. Jejunal, ileal, and colonic perforations tend to produce gas below the transverse mesocolon.
[Both] Small bowel perforation often produces only a small pneumoperitoneum, while gastric perforation, colonic perforation, and misplaced feeding tubes may produce large or massive free air.
[Both] Incidental free intraperitoneal air in an asymptomatic patient should prompt consideration of benign causes, especially postoperative air and benign pneumatosis-related rupture.

=== CAUSE-SPECIFIC CLUES ===
[Both] Normal postoperative pneumoperitoneum: a small amount of free air is expected after laparotomy or invasive peritoneal procedures such as peritoneal dialysis or gastrostomy-tube placement. It is seen on CT in nearly 90% of patients at 3 days and about 50% at 6 days. It usually resolves in 7-10 days but can persist longer after prior surgery or peritonitis. Large or massive postoperative free air is abnormal and should raise concern for anastomotic leak or perforation.
[Both] Barotrauma: positive-pressure ventilation can cause alveolar rupture, pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, subcutaneous emphysema, pneumatosis, and pneumoperitoneum. Pleural or mediastinal gas may dissect into the retroperitoneum or peritoneum. Suspect this when pneumothorax and pneumoperitoneum coexist in a ventilated patient.
[Both] Iatrogenic injury: endoscopy, feeding-tube placement, and postoperative bowel anastomoses are key contexts. Endoscopy may produce free air even without frank perforation if the bowel becomes markedly distended. Anastomotic leak appears as greater-than-expected postoperative free air, often with peritonitis or abscess.
[Both] Perforated ulcer: gastroduodenal ulcers are the most common cause of GI tract perforation. Duodenal perforation usually causes gas, fluid, and fat stranding near the duodenal bulb and may involve both intraperitoneal and anterior pararenal spaces. Gastric ulcer perforation often involves the lesser sac, with gas/fluid/contrast posterior to the stomach and spread through the foramen of Winslow.
[Both] Diverticulitis: a common pelvic cause. Because the omentum often walls off the perforation, the amount of free air is usually small and adjacent to the inflamed sigmoid colon. Large pneumoperitoneum and generalized peritonitis can occur, especially in older adults or steroid users.
[Both] Intestinal trauma: pneumoperitoneum after blunt trauma indicates intestinal perforation until proven otherwise, with the duodenum and proximal jejunum important sites. Look for mesenteric hematoma and bowel wall thickening. Diagnostic peritoneal lavage can itself introduce intraperitoneal air and fluid.
[Both] Appendicitis: rupture usually produces only a small amount of free air, often with free fluid or abscess.
[Both] Thoracic mimics on radiographs: basal pneumothorax, plate-like subsegmental atelectasis, and cystic/bullous lung disease near the diaphragm may simulate pneumoperitoneum.
[Both] Colonic interposition: Chilaiditi configuration places colon between liver and right hemidiaphragm and may be mistaken for free air.
[Both] Subphrenic fat: radiolucent subxiphoid or perihepatic fat can mimic free air on radiographs.
[Both] Peritonitis and abscess: gas-forming pyogenic infection can produce intraperitoneal gas/fluid; look for peritoneal thickening/enhancement or abscess. A gas-containing subphrenic abscess can simulate free abdominal gas on upright radiograph.
[Both] Pneumatosis cystoides intestinalis: benign cystic intramural gas, often associated with scleroderma/collagen vascular disease or steroid/immunosuppressive therapy, can rupture and produce pneumoperitoneum despite a clinically well patient.
[Both] Female genital tract: gas may pass retrograde into the peritoneum after sexual intercourse, pelvic examination, or water sports.
[Both] Small bowel diverticula: duodenal or small-intestinal diverticula can perforate spontaneously or after feeding-tube placement. They usually produce very little free air and may not require surgery if contained and clinically mild.
[Both] Foreign body perforation: ingested bone, toothpick, pin, or similar objects tend to perforate at the ileum, ileocecal region, or rectosigmoid.
