Richter Hernia

Parth J. Patel; Christopher Pfeifer.

Continuing Education Activity

A Richter hernia involves the herniation of the anti-mesenteric portion of the intestine through a fascial defect, often presenting with symptoms like abdominal discomfort, distention, nausea, and vomiting. The incidence of Richter hernias has increased with the rise of minimally invasive surgery. Diagnosis is challenging and requires a high index of suspicion, thorough clinical history, physical examination, and imaging. Treatment typically involves surgical intervention to assess and address bowel viability, which may include resection and fascial defect repair. This course educates clinicians on the evaluation and treatment of Richter hernias, emphasizing the importance of an interprofessional approach to patient care. Collaborative efforts among clinicians, surgeons, radiologists, and other healthcare professionals improve diagnosis accuracy, decision-making, and patient outcomes, ensuring comprehensive management of this rare yet potentially life-threatening condition.

Objectives:

  • Differentiate Richter hernias from other causes of abdominal pain and hernia subtypes by understanding the pathophysiology of antimesenteric wall involvement and its implications for delayed diagnosis and strangulation risk.
  • Assess patients for signs of strangulation or ischemia, including evolving pain, systemic toxicity, and localized peritonitis, recognizing that progression may be insidious but carries significant morbidity and mortality.
  • Implement timely diagnostic and management strategies, including early surgical consultation, when Richter hernia is suspected, especially in the setting of concerning clinical features such as tachycardia, localized tenderness, or systemic signs.
  • Collaborate with emergency medicine, radiology, anesthesia, and surgical teams to ensure rapid evaluation, risk stratification, and operative readiness for patients with suspected or confirmed Richter hernia.

Introduction

A Richter hernia is a lesser-known type of hernia. Although not well known and overall rare, Richter hernias can lead to grave clinical sequelae. A Richter hernia is a herniation of the bowel's antimesenteric portion through a fascial defect; this mechanism helps explain the often subclinical symptoms and late presentation. With the advent of minimally invasive surgery, there has been an increase in the incidence of Richter hernias, which has only continued as minimally invasive surgeries have become more popular. Operative treatment of Richter hernias depends on the viability of the involved bowel and may often require bowel resection in addition to repair of the fascial defect.   

The Richter hernia was named after August Gottlich Richter, who described it in 1785. Before this date, there were reported cases of a fascial defect that involved the antimesenteric portion of the bowel wall. Fabricius Hildanus first reported a case like this in 1598, which was followed by a few reports by Alexis Littre in 1700. Richter was the first to describe this hernia as a “partial enterocele.” As time passed, others, such as Antonio Scarpa, further defined the pathophysiology. By 1887, Frederick Treves reported a case series of such hernias.[1][2]

Etiology

Richter hernias occur when defects or weaknesses in the abdominal wall allow intra-abdominal contents, most often the small intestine, to protrude partially. This includes incisional hernias, and the growing use of minimally invasive surgical techniques has contributed to a modest increase in incisional Richter hernias (see Image. Richter Hernia on Computed Tomography).[3]

Epidemiology

Richter hernias typically occur in those aged 60 to 80; however, they can theoretically appear at any age, and pediatric case reports exist. There is a slight increase in incidence among women. This preference may reflect that femoral hernias are more common in women than men, and the femoral ring is the most frequent site for a Richter hernia.

Estimates suggest that about 10% of all hernias are Richter hernias, though this may be under-appreciated.[2] The most common locations for Richter hernias are the femoral canal (36%-88%), followed by the inguinal canal (12%-36%) and abdominal wall incisional hernias (4%-25%).[2][4] As stated above, the increase in laparoscopic and robotic-assisted procedures has led to a rise in Richter hernias because of unclosed port sites, allowing for herniation of a portion of the bowel wall through the small fascial defect.

Pathophysiology

By definition, a Richter hernia is a herniation of only a portion of the circumference of the bowel wall through the fascial defect. Most commonly, it is the antimesenteric portion of the bowel. These hernias often develop in small fascial defects, which must be large enough for a portion of the bowel to protrude through, but not large enough to accommodate the entire circumference of the bowel.

In many cases, the involved bowel segment is the terminal ileum. The ensuing process results from the incarceration of a portion of the bowel wall. Incarceration of the intestine can lead to strangulation of the blood supply to that portion of the bowel, followed by edema and venous congestion, leading to rapid segmental ischemia and gangrene with a reported incidence of necrosis of 69% at the time of operative intervention.[2][5]

History and Physical

Similar to the presentation of other incarcerated hernias, patients often present with abdominal discomfort, distention, nausea, and vomiting. Because this hernia involves only a portion of the bowel wall, there is no complete obstruction of the intestinal lumen. Obstructive symptoms rarely present if less than two-thirds of the bowel wall is involved. Lack of complete obstruction often leads to subclinical symptoms for a period until the process becomes advanced and there is strangulation of the involved bowel, resulting in an intensification of the above symptoms.

Upon presentation, it is essential to obtain a complete history and physical with particular attention to predisposing factors, such as previous hernias and a history of minimally invasive surgery. A comprehensive medical history should be obtained in detail, including comorbid conditions, active cardiopulmonary disease, and anticoagulant use. When surgical intervention is required, patients may need additional treatment, such as reversal of anticoagulation or cardiac risk stratification based on comorbid conditions.

Depending on the size of the defect and viability of the bowel, physical exam findings will range from a completely benign abdomen to frank peritonitis. Special attention should be placed on any surgical scars or masses. If a hernia or hernia defect cannot be palpated, the Valsalva maneuver may be used to increase intra-abdominal pressure, making the presence of any hernia more obvious and allowing it to be palpated. In cases of advanced ischemia or necrosis, the abdomen may be rigid and/or diffusely tender.[3]

Evaluation

Ultrasound and computed tomography scans are useful adjuncts in diagnosis. However, because a Richter hernia involves only a portion of the bowel wall, these imaging modalities can yield false-negative results.[6] Laboratory data can raise suspicion for but cannot confirm a diagnosis of Richter hernia. Complete blood count can be obtained to evaluate for leukocytosis and thrombocytopenia. Basic metabolic panel and lactate level can be obtained to evaluate for electrolyte derangements, dehydration, and evidence of hypoperfusion. Coagulation studies should be obtained to assess for coagulopathy that may require preoperative correction in patients with hepatic dysfunction or on anticoagulation.

Treatment / Management

Treatment of Richter hernias depends on clinical status, physical examination, and suspicion of strangulation. Clinically stable patients with reducible hernias should undergo elective repair. Strangulated hernias of any type are a surgical emergency, and Richter hernias do not deviate from this principle. Immediate operative exploration is required. Simultaneously, resuscitation and treatment of sepsis should also be undertaken with intravenous fluids and antibiotics.[7]

The surgical approach depends on several factors, including the hernia's location, the patient's clinical condition, and the surgeon's preference. An open surgical procedure may be the best option for patients with evidence of hemodynamic instability, obstruction, or strangulation. The incision placement varies based on the hernia's location. Midline laparotomy is generally suitable for ventral incisional hernias. Femoral and inguinal Richter hernias might require an inguinal incision along with a counterlaparotomy incision.

Minimally invasive hernia repair using either laparoscopic or robotic techniques can also be considered. Because these methods require the establishment of pneumoperitoneum, they may be more suitable for hemodynamically stable individuals, although there is no absolute contraindication to laparoscopic surgery. Dilated bowel loops might prevent safe laparoscopic access and limit space for pneumoperitoneum. Hemodynamic instability can worsen in the presence of pneumoperitoneum due to reduced venous return. Therefore, a minimally invasive approach is often ideal for both urgent and elective case settings.[8] Transabdominal preperitoneal and total extraperitoneal approaches may be considered for inguinal or femoral defects. Various minimally invasive methods for ventral hernia repair are available, including preperitoneal and intraperitoneal approaches.

Regardless of the surgical approach, surgeons must carefully evaluate bowel viability. Any questionable bowel should be resected and anastomosed. Some groups have suggested that if the ischemic area is a small, coin-shaped lesion involving less than half of the bowel wall circumference, it may be invaginated, and the new edges sutured and reapproximated.[5][9] The bowel may be evaluated for color and peristalsis, although these findings do not necessarily indicate viability.[10] Methods to assess bowel viability include intravenous fluorescein injection and Wood lamp examination. A more modern approach to the Wood lamp involves the intravenous injection of indocyanine green, followed by infrared imaging angiography.[11] One may also assess for arterial flow using a Doppler. 

Repair of a hernia defect depends on the size of the defect and the wound class. The current standard of care for repairing most hernias, including femoral, inguinal, and incisional hernias, involves placing a prosthetic mesh. However, because Richter hernias often involve strangulated bowel requiring resection, mesh placement is controversial. Ultimately, the decision to place mesh and the type of mesh used depend on the surgeon’s clinical judgment. Recent studies suggest that placing prosthetic mesh in contaminated fields is safe and can reduce recurrence rates, although the rate of surgical site infection is higher. Biologic mesh has a lower rate of surgical site infection in contaminated fields but is also more expensive and associated with higher recurrence rates.[12][13]

Differential Diagnosis

When evaluating Richter hernias, the clinician must maintain a broad differential diagnosis. As previously discussed, patients with this condition often present with abdominal pain. They rarely have symptoms of complete obstruction and may have vague symptoms.

Differential diagnosis should include abdominal wall masses such as lipomas and abscesses. Consideration should be given to ileus and bowel obstruction, along with their potential underlying causes (ie, adhesions, hernias, intraluminal masses), which are often unknown until surgical intervention, regardless of whether a patient has a Richter hernia. Thus, the differential diagnosis should include a strangulated hernia involving the complete bowel lumen, especially if presenting with sepsis and acute obstruction.

Prognosis

After successful hernia repair, there is always the potential for recurrence. Modifiable patient risk factors for recurrence include elevated body mass index, smoking, diabetes, and steroid use. Risk factors related to surgery and technique include surgical site infection, seroma formation, tissue overlap, and surgeon experience.[14] As Richter hernias are often incisional hernias from prior procedures, there is a particular risk of recurrence with the repair of these defects, estimated at 11%, with obesity as the most significant risk factor.[15]

Complications

Because of the initial lack of obstructive symptoms, Richter hernias may undergo chronic incarceration and, if left unaddressed, may present as an enterocutaneous fistula. Various published case reports with rare presentations of Richter hernias have included a colocutaneous fistula after inguinal hernia repair and a laparoscopic gastric bypass.[16][17] These complicated presentations often require more extensive repair and recovery due to laparotomy and takedown of the fistula. 

Postoperative and Rehabilitation Care

Recovery after Richter hernia repair centers on modifying risk factors for recurrence. Patients should be advised to refrain from smoking and heavy lifting. Continued attention to weight is a strong recommendation, as obesity is a risk factor for the development of hernias. An abdominal binder can be used in the postoperative period for comfort, but the patient's respiratory status should be considered when recommending a binder.

Deterrence and Patient Education

Deterrence and patient education in the context of a Richter hernia center on early recognition, risk modification, and setting appropriate perioperative expectations, as delayed presentation is a major driver of morbidity, given the propensity for strangulation without overt obstruction. Preoperatively, the surgeon should provide a clear explanation of the planned procedure, anticipated recovery, and expected postoperative pain, while also emphasizing the unique risk profile of Richter hernias, namely, the potential for bowel wall ischemia despite minimal or absent obstructive symptoms. Patients should be explicitly counseled on warning signs warranting urgent evaluation, including localized, persistent pain at the hernia site, overlying skin changes, nausea, or systemic symptoms such as fever, even in the absence of classic obstructive features.

Informed consent should include discussion of operative risks such as bleeding, infection, injury to surrounding structures, recurrence, and the possibility of intraoperative findings necessitating bowel resection. Patients should understand that, due to the partial circumference involvement characteristic of Richter hernias, compromised bowel may not be apparent until exploration, and resection with anastomosis may be required. Accordingly, risks specific to resection, such as anastomotic leak, intra-abdominal sepsis, or the need for reoperation or second-look surgery, should be reviewed. In patients with high-risk comorbidities, including those on immunosuppressive therapy or with underlying inflammatory bowel disease, the discussion should also address the potential need for temporary or permanent ostomy formation.

From a deterrence standpoint, modifiable risk factors should be addressed both pre- and postoperatively. Patients should be counseled to avoid activities that acutely increase intra-abdominal pressure, such as heavy lifting or straining, particularly during the recovery period, with typical lifting restrictions extending for 4 to 6 weeks postoperatively. Smoking cessation should be strongly encouraged due to its adverse effects on wound healing and increased risk of hernia recurrence. Optimization of comorbid conditions, such as glycemic control in people with diabetes and nutritional status in chronically ill patients, also plays a critical role in reducing complications and recurrence risk. Finally, reinforcing adherence to follow-up and educating patients on early symptom recognition are essential components in preventing delayed diagnosis and mitigating the potentially severe consequences of strangulated Richter hernias.

Enhancing Healthcare Team Outcomes

Effective management of Richter hernias requires a coordinated, high-reliability team approach anchored in early recognition, decisive surgical strategy, and disciplined communication across care settings. Because these hernias often involve only the antimesenteric wall and may not produce frank obstruction, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion, particularly in patients with vague, localized abdominal pain and a history of minimally invasive surgery. Primary care and urgent care clinicians play a critical front-line role by recognizing subtle presentations and expediting referral.

In the emergency department, physicians and advanced clinicians should prioritize early surgical consultation when incarceration or strangulation is suspected; given the reported mortality of up to 17% with strangulated Richter hernias, surgeons may appropriately proceed to operative intervention based on clinical findings rather than delay for imaging. Clear, closed-loop communication between emergency department staff and the surgical team, explicitly conveying concern for strangulation, exam evolution, and resuscitation status, reduces time to definitive care and mitigates risk. Perioperatively, anesthesiologists and operating room teams must anticipate hemodynamic instability, particularly in septic patients, using goal-directed resuscitation and vigilant monitoring.

Postoperatively, coordinated care among surgeons, intensivists or hospitalists, nurses, pharmacists, and rehabilitation specialists is essential to optimize outcomes. Nursing staff provide continuous assessment for early signs of deterioration (eg, evolving sepsis, wound complications), reinforce activity restrictions, and deliver patient-centered education. Pharmacists contribute to antimicrobial stewardship, analgesia optimization, and medication reconciliation, especially in medically complex patients. Care coordination should include structured handoffs, daily multidisciplinary rounds, and early discharge planning with clear follow-up pathways and return precautions. Across all roles, integrating technical expertise with respectful, transparent communication fosters situational awareness, accelerates decision-making, and ultimately enhances patient safety, recovery, and team performance.
