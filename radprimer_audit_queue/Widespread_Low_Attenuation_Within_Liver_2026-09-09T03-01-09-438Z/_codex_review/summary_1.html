🧭 Widespread Low Attenuation Within Liver

Source Basis: Core + RadPrimer + STATdx synthesis. The pattern is approached by separating true fat from edema, congestion, ischemia/necrosis, diffuse tumor infiltration, granulomatous/infectious disease, and treatment-related injury.
🖼️ Key Imaging Findings
  • First discriminator: mass effect is usually absent in steatosis and more concerning for neoplastic infiltration.
  • Steatosis: may be diffuse, geographic, or multifocal; vessels can traverse low-density regions without displacement. Focal fat commonly occurs near the gallbladder fossa, falciform/subcapsular region, and periportal spaces.
  • CT steatosis: decreased attenuation relative to spleen; unenhanced CT is more reliable than contrast CT for fat assessment.
  • MRI steatosis: signal loss on opposed-phase GRE is the key confirmation maneuver because mixed fat and water signals cancel when out of phase.
  • Ultrasound steatosis: increased hepatic echogenicity relative to renal cortex with increased sound attenuation.
  • Hepatitis: imaging is often normal; when abnormal, periportal edema and gallbladder wall edema are typical. Massive necrosis can produce diffuse low attenuation.
  • Passive congestion: dilated hepatic veins/IVC with refluxed contrast and mottled or nutmeg enhancement; hepatomegaly and ascites may accompany it.
  • Budd-Chiari: hepatic venous/IVC thrombus or absent venous flow plus caudate sparing/hypertrophy, peripheral injury, and collateralization are key clues.
  • Diffuse neoplastic infiltration: metastases or lymphoma can mimic steatosis on noncontrast CT; focal lesions, splenic disease, lymphadenopathy, mass effect, contrast behavior, ultrasound, or MRI can expose the infiltrative process.
  • Metastases: most are hypovascular and best seen on portal venous phase; melanoma is a hypervascular primary. MRI commonly shows T1-low/T2-high lesions, while melanoma can be T1 high from melanin or blood products.
  • Lymphoma: may diffusely infiltrate the liver, often with splenomegaly/adenopathy; CT/MRI are typically hypoenhancing and MRI may restrict diffusion.
  • Granulomatous/infectious disease: sarcoidosis may produce hepatic and splenic low-density granulomas with adenopathy; opportunistic fungal infection may produce tiny hypoenhancing/T2-bright microabscesses.
  • Infiltrative HCC: can resemble steatosis on noncontrast CT; heterogeneity, hypervascular foci, washout, mass effect, and vascular invasion increase concern.
  • Radiation injury: a sharply demarcated low-attenuation region matching the treatment field is a major discriminator.

📌 Pearls / Pitfalls
  • Diffuse low attenuation is not synonymous with fat: toxic necrosis, lymphoma, metastases, hepatitis, congestion, and vascular injury may mimic steatosis.
  • When CT attenuation is ambiguous, chemical-shift MRI can directly confirm microscopic fat; companion contrast, ultrasound, or MR sequences may reveal infiltrative disease.
  • Do not generalize single-case outcomes or disease associations beyond what is explicitly shown in the source case.

🧾 Key Measurements / Criteria
  • Core traditional unenhanced CT criterion for steatosis: liver attenuation at least 10 HU below spleen; Core also notes newer work suggesting even 1 HU relative hypoattenuation may represent steatosis.
  • Core portal-venous-phase criterion for diffuse hypoattenuation: liver at least 25 HU below spleen, with lower reliability than unenhanced CT.

⭐ Super Summary
When the liver is diffusely or geographically low in attenuation, first decide whether there is true fat. Preserved vessel course and opposed-phase signal loss support steatosis; mass effect, focal lesions, abnormal vascular flow, contrast-phase abnormalities, adenopathy/splenic disease, edema/ascites, or treatment-field boundaries redirect the differential.
